A heart beating, measured from one CT study

A gated cardiac CT records the same heart at 21 moments of a single heartbeat. A network finds the heart in one of those moments, registration carries that outline to the other 20, and what you see below moves the way this patient's heart moved. Nothing here is keyframed by hand.

What this does not claim

No volumes, and no ejection fraction. Both were tempting and both would have been wrong. The scan's field of view cuts the great vessels mid-course, so the heart surface it produces has 1,770 open boundary edges: it is not a closed surface, and the volume inside an open surface is undefined. The first attempt at measuring it returned numbers that moved by tens of per cent between two segmentations of the same heart, which is the signature of a leaking mesh rather than of a beating heart.

Separating left from right, and ventricle from atrium, needs TotalSegmentator's heartchambers_highres model, which is licensed separately and free for academic use on request. With those labels the chambers can be closed and measured properly. Until then this page shows motion, which it measured, and stays quiet about volume, which it did not.

How it was built

  1. The study

    A gated 4D cardiac CT of a truncal valve case, released by the Jolley Lab at Children's Hospital of Philadelphia through SlicerHeart and distributed with MONAI Physio. 21 phases, 512 by 391 by 218 voxels at 0.6 by 0.6 by 0.7 mm, 2.5 GB.

  2. Finding the heart

    TotalSegmentator v2 through MONAI Physio's SegmentChestTotalSegmentatorWithContrast, which detects contrast enhancement and adjusts its thresholds for it. 58 structures in 400 seconds on an RTX 4050. The fast 3 mm model does the same job in 42 seconds, but its wall was coarse enough to be worth the extra six minutes, and only the reference phase is segmented so the cost is paid once. The heart came back as one structure, because the six-way chamber split is a separately licensed model.

  3. Measuring the motion

    Greedy diffeomorphic registration with normalised cross correlation, four resolution levels at 30, 15, 7 and 3 iterations, warping each of the other 20 phases onto phase 15. The deformation fields are the measured displacement of tissue, and they are what makes this a reconstruction rather than an animation. 19.8 minutes for the full series.

  4. Fitting a browser

    The result is a 236 MB animated USD built for Omniverse. Getting it into a web page meant decimating the heart surface by 79% and the aorta by 27% while keeping vertex correspondence across phases, which vtkDecimatePro allows because it deletes vertices rather than relocating them. The surviving indices are taken from every phase and written as glTF morph targets, so the browser interpolates between phases instead of stepping through them. 8.2 MB.

What ran out of memory first

The first attempt held all 21 phases at native resolution and the Linux out-of-memory killer took the process at phase 6, at 23.9 GB resident. The fix cost no accuracy: the segmenter resamples its input to 1 mm isotropic before inference anyway, so the finer sampling was being discarded. Resampling the series first cut the voxel count to a quarter, and the run finished in 18 minutes rather than the projected 70.
