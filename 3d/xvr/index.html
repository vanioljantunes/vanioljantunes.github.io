X-ray to volume registration

How it works

A digitally reconstructed radiograph is a line integral. For every detector pixel a ray leaves the X-ray source, crosses the CT volume and lands on the detector, and the pixel value is the sum of the linear attenuation coefficient along that ray. The fragment shader on this page marches each ray in 384 equal steps through a 3D texture holding the CT, which is the forward model that DiffDRR (opens in a new tab) implements in PyTorch.

Registration then asks which of the six rigid pose parameters — three rotations and three translations of the C-arm about the isocentre — produce a DRR that looks like the fixed radiograph. Similarity is normalised cross-correlation over the whole image. The optimiser is Adam on the gradient of one minus that correlation, and the gradient comes from central differences: each iteration renders thirteen poses into one tiled framebuffer, reads it back once, and computes thirteen correlations. It searches coarse first, at 64 by 64 pixels, then refines at 112 by 112.

The fixed radiograph is itself a DRR, rendered at a pose drawn at random and hidden from the optimiser. That is the standard synthetic-target setup for measuring registration accuracy: because the true pose is known, the rotation and translation errors above are real errors rather than estimates.

What this is not

The contribution of XVR is a neural network that regresses the pose directly, trained per patient in minutes, which removes the need for a good starting guess and makes the method fast enough for the operating room. That network is PyTorch and CUDA and does not run in a browser. What runs here is the differentiable-rendering half, the part xvr register uses to refine a pose, started from a deliberately perturbed one. Drag the sliders far enough and the failure mode the paper is about appears: the correlation surface has local maxima, and gradient descent alone settles into the wrong one.
